# Chapter 1 (DA): Analysis environment and descriptive statistics

Almost every decision in modern life is backed by "the numbers": the average salary of a graduate, the typical delivery time, the worst-case response time of a website, the marks that separate a pass from a distinction. Most of those numbers are a **summary**: hundreds or millions of values squeezed into one or two figures. A summary is useful because it is short. It is dangerous for the same reason: whatever the short version leaves out, you cannot see any more.

This chapter teaches you to produce summaries that are **honest**. You will learn the tools (a notebook for exploring, a script for repeating), the vocabulary (mean, median, mode, variance, percentile, skew), and the habits that separate an analyst from someone who just presses "describe". By the end you reach this outcome: **you can summarise a dataset, and you can explain, with numbers, when the median is a better summary than the mean.**

We use only Python's standard library here: `statistics`, `csv`, `math`, `random`, `collections`, `pathlib` and `json`. NumPy and pandas start in the next chapters (da.02 and da.03). That is on purpose: if you compute a variance by hand once, you will never be confused by what `df.var()` does later.

Prerequisites: core.12 and everything before it (functions, lists, dictionaries, files, modules, exceptions, comprehensions). No statistics background is assumed.

## 1. Analysis starts with a question

### 1.1 Why a question first?

Here is a dataset: 200 monthly household incomes. A beginner opens it and asks, "What can I calculate?" They compute the mean, the maximum, a histogram, and get a page of numbers that answer nothing, because no question was asked.

A better habit: **write the question in one sentence before you touch the data.**

- "Can a typical household in this town afford a 55-lakh house?" (needs a typical income, and a sense of how many are far below it.)
- "Is the website fast enough?" (needs a definition of "fast enough": the average user? 99 out of 100 users?)
- "Did the new teaching method help?" (needs a comparison, not a single average.)

The question decides which summary is right. "The typical household" asks for a centre. "99 out of 100 users" asks for a high percentile. "Did it help" asks for two groups side by side. The same numbers give different answers to different questions, and none of those answers is wrong, except the one you gave without knowing the question.

### 1.2 The loop of analysis

Most real analysis is the same small loop, repeated:

```text
question -> get data -> look at it (raw!) -> clean -> summarise -> check the summary against the raw data -> answer, with the caveats
     ^                                                                                                          |
     +----------------------------------- the answer raises a new question -------------------------------------+
```

Two steps beginners skip are "look at the raw data" and "check the summary against the raw data". We will do both in every section: print some rows, draw the shape, and only then trust a number.

### 1.3 Descriptive and inferential statistics

This chapter is **descriptive** statistics: describing the data you have (the 200 incomes in the file). The other half, **inferential** statistics, asks what a sample says about a bigger population you did not measure (do all households in the town earn this?). Inference needs probability and comes much later in your path. Descriptive statistics comes first because inference is built from it, and because many good analyses never need more than a careful description.

One word to fix now: a **population** is all the cases you care about; a **sample** is the part you actually measured. The difference matters for one formula in this chapter (the variance, section 11.3), so keep it in mind.

### 1.4 Vocabulary you will use all chapter

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| observation (row, case) | one thing measured | one household |
| variable (column, field) | one property measured on every case | monthly income |
| dataset | all observations of some variables | a CSV of 200 households |
| statistic | a number computed from a sample | the mean income of the 200 |
| distribution | how the values are spread over the possible values | most incomes near 30,000, a few above 100,000 |
| outlier | a value far from the rest | an income of 134,000 when most are below 70,000 |

## 2. Notebooks: Jupyter and VS Code

### 2.1 What a notebook is

A **notebook** is a document made of **cells**. A cell is either **code** (Python you can run) or **markdown** (text, headings, formulas). When you run a code cell, the result (printed text, a table, a chart) appears right under it and is saved in the document. The result is a document that mixes your question, your code, your output and your explanation, in the order you thought about them. That is why analysts love notebooks: exploring is a conversation with the data, and the notebook records the conversation.

A notebook has two parts working together:

- the **document** (the `.ipynb` file, what you see and edit), and
- the **kernel**: a separate running Python process that actually executes your cells and **remembers** the variables between them.

The kernel's memory is the source of both the power and the danger of notebooks (section 3).

### 2.2 Jupyter and VS Code

"Jupyter" is the project that defined the notebook format and the kernel protocol. You can use it in two common ways:

| Way | What it is | How you start it |
|---|---|---|
| JupyterLab / Jupyter Notebook | a program that opens notebooks in your web browser | `jupyter lab` in a terminal |
| VS Code notebooks | notebooks inside your editor, using the *Jupyter* extension | create or open a `.ipynb` file in VS Code |

They read and write the same `.ipynb` files, so you can switch freely. VS Code has a big advantage for you: you already use it for Python, with its debugger, its search and its Git panel, and the notebook sits inside the same window.

### 2.5 What an `.ipynb` file really is

A notebook file is **JSON**. We can build and read one with the standard library, which takes away the mystery. (The file goes to a temporary folder and is deleted at the end.)

```python run
import json
import tempfile
from pathlib import Path

notebook = {
    "nbformat": 4,
    "nbformat_minor": 5,
    "metadata": {"kernelspec": {"name": "python3", "display_name": "Python 3"}},
    "cells": [
        {"cell_type": "markdown", "metadata": {}, "source": ["# Incomes\n", "First look."]},

            "cell_type": "code",
            "metadata": {},

            "source": ["data = [3, 5, 9]\n", "sum(data) / len(data)"],
            "outputs": [
                {
                    "output_type": "execute_result",
                    "execution_count": 1,
                    "metadata": {},
                    "data": {"text/plain": ["5.666666666666667"]},
                }
            ],
        },
    ],
}

with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as folder:
    path = Path(folder) / "first.ipynb"
    path.write_text(json.dumps(notebook, indent=1), encoding="utf-8")
    print("file size:", path.stat().st_size, "bytes")
    loaded = json.loads(path.read_text(encoding="utf-8"))

print("top-level keys:", sorted(loaded))
for number, cell in enumerate(loaded["cells"], start=1):
    kind = cell["cell_type"]
    first_line = cell["source"][0].rstrip("\n")
```

```text
file size: 611 bytes
top-level keys: ['cells', 'metadata', 'nbformat', 'nbformat_minor']
cell 1: markdown first line = '# Incomes'            has saved output = False

```

Three consequences follow from this:

1. **Outputs are stored in the file.** If you send the notebook to a friend, they see your results without running anything. But the outputs also make diffs noisy in Git, and a notebook with a huge chart in it is a huge file.
2. **Numbers like `execution_count`** change every time you run a cell, so even a harmless re-run changes the file. Teams therefore often strip outputs before committing (a separate tool called `nbstripout` does this; you would install it yourself).
3. **A notebook is data, not a program.** Python does not run `.ipynb` files. The kernel runs the cells *you* choose, in the order *you* choose. That freedom is exactly what causes the trap in the next section.

## 3. The notebook trap: hidden state

### 3.1 The kernel remembers everything

The kernel keeps variables alive between cells. That is wonderful for exploring (load a big file once, then try ten ideas), and it is the number-one source of wrong results in notebooks. The reason: **the order in which cells are *run* is not the order in which they appear on the page.** The page can say one thing while the kernel's memory says another.

We can show this with a 15-line imitation of a kernel. Each cell is a string; running a cell executes it in one shared dictionary, exactly what a kernel does.

```python run
class MiniKernel:
    """A toy kernel: cells share one namespace, and we count the runs."""

    def __init__(self):
        self.namespace = {}
        self.run_count = 0

    def run(self, source):
        self.run_count += 1
        print(f"[{self.run_count}] >>> {source}")
        exec(source, self.namespace)

    def restart(self):
        self.namespace = {}
        self.run_count = 0
        print("--- kernel restarted: all variables forgotten ---")


# The three cells as they appear on the page, top to bottom:
page = [
    "price = 50",
    "total = price * 3",
    "print('total is', total)",
]

kernel = MiniKernel()
for cell in page:
    kernel.run(cell)
```
```text
[1] >>> price = 50
[2] >>> total = price * 3
[3] >>> print('total is', total)
total is 150
```

So far all is well: run top to bottom, `total is 150`. Now you decide the price is wrong. You scroll up, edit the first cell to `price = 80`, and run **only that cell**. Then you go to the bottom and run the print cell again:

```python run
class MiniKernel:
    def __init__(self):
        self.namespace = {}
        self.run_count = 0

    def run(self, source):
        self.run_count += 1
        print(f"[{self.run_count}] >>> {source}")
        exec(source, self.namespace)

    def restart(self):
        self.namespace = {}
        self.run_count = 0
        print("--- kernel restarted: all variables forgotten ---")


kernel = MiniKernel()
kernel.run("price = 50")
kernel.run("total = price * 3")
kernel.run("print('total is', total)")

print()
print("You edit cell 1 to 'price = 80' and run only that cell, then the last cell:")
kernel.run("price = 80")
kernel.run("print('total is', total)")

print()
print("Restart and run the page top to bottom, as a reader would:")
kernel.restart()
for cell in ["price = 80", "total = price * 3", "print('total is', total)"]:
    kernel.run(cell)
```
```text
[1] >>> price = 50
[2] >>> total = price * 3
[3] >>> print('total is', total)
total is 150

You edit cell 1 to 'price = 80' and run only that cell, then the last cell:
[4] >>> price = 80
[5] >>> print('total is', total)
total is 150

Restart and run the page top to bottom, as a reader would:
--- kernel restarted: all variables forgotten ---
[1] >>> price = 80
[2] >>> total = price * 3
[3] >>> print('total is', total)
total is 240
```

The page now says `price = 80` and the screen shows `total is 150`. **Both are "true"**: 150 was computed from the old price, which the kernel remembered. A reader who runs the notebook from the top gets 240. If you had published 150, your report would be wrong and nobody, including you next week, could tell why.

### 3.2 Other ways hidden state hurts

- **Deleted cells.** You delete the cell that defined `rate`, but the kernel still remembers `rate`. Everything works, until you restart.
- **Cells run out of order.** Cell 7 uses a variable defined in cell 9. It works because you happened to run 9 first.
- **Mutating shared data.** Cell 3 sorts the list in place; cell 4 assumes it is unsorted. Run cell 3 twice and its result changes.
- **Re-running appends.** `data.append(x)` in a cell gives different results each time you run it.

### 3.3 The three habits that fix it

1. **Restart and run all** before you trust, share or commit a notebook. In Jupyter: *Kernel > Restart Kernel and Run All Cells*; in VS Code: the *Restart* button, then *Run All*. If it fails or shows different numbers, you had hidden state. Do this at least before you finish each session.
2. **Make cells depend only on earlier cells.** Read the notebook top to bottom in your head; each name used must be defined above.
3. **Prefer functions to loose statements.** A function does not leak its temporary variables into the kernel, and it can be moved to a `.py` file (section 4).

Rule of thumb: *a notebook that cannot be restarted and run top to bottom is broken, however good it looks.*

### 3.4 Side-effect traps with mutation

The same hidden-state problem exists in plain Python when a cell mutates data. Here is the sorted-in-place version, run "twice" the way you would re-run a cell:

```python run
data = [30, 10, 20]

def median_wrong(values):
    values.sort()                 # mutates the caller's list!
    return values[len(values) // 2]

print("median:", median_wrong(data))
print("your data afterwards:", data)

def median_right(values):
    ordered = sorted(values)      # a new list; the original is untouched
    return ordered[len(ordered) // 2]

fresh = [30, 10, 20]
print("median:", median_right(fresh))
print("your data afterwards:", fresh)
```
```text
median: 20
your data afterwards: [10, 20, 30]
median: 20
your data afterwards: [30, 10, 20]
```

(`median_right` is only right for odd-length lists; the full rule is in section 8.) The lesson for analysis: **never let a helper silently reorder or modify your data.** Use `sorted()`, not `.sort()`, on data you still need.

## 4. Notebook or script?

### 4.1 They are for different jobs

| Question | Notebook | Script (`.py`) |
|---|---|---|
| Exploring unknown data, trying ideas | best | slow |
| Showing the story: question, code, chart, conclusion | best | poor |
| Running the same job every day | poor | best |
| Testing with `pytest` | awkward | easy |
| Code review and Git history | noisy (JSON with outputs) | clean |
| Hidden-state risk | high | none (each run starts fresh) |
| Reuse from other files (`import`) | no | yes |

### 4.2 The workflow professionals use

1. **Explore in a notebook** (or `# %%` file): load, look, try things, make charts. Messy is fine.
2. **When a piece of code works and you would copy it, move it into a `.py` module** as a function with a docstring and tests.
3. **The notebook then imports it** (`from stats_tools import describe`) and keeps only the story: question, call, output, interpretation.
4. **Anything that must run on a schedule or on new data becomes a script** with `argparse` or a `main()` (see the automation chapter, swe.11).

Notice that this chapter follows the same workflow: the `datasets.py` file in section 5 and the `describe()` function in section 17 are *modules*, and every section after them could be a notebook cell that imports them.

### 4.3 A checklist for a notebook you will share

- Title cell: the question, the data source, the date, the author.
- Imports and settings in the first code cell.
- Restart and run all: passes, no errors, same numbers.
- No cell depends on a file path only your computer has (use a relative path or a variable at the top).
- A closing markdown cell: the answer in plain words, and its limits.

## 5. Our practice datasets

### 5.1 Why synthetic data, and what that means

We need data with realistic shapes: incomes that are right-skewed, response times with a heavy tail, marks that cluster around a middle. Real data of that kind needs a download, and this course works offline. So the module below **generates** five datasets with Python's `random` module, using fixed seeds. The numbers are **invented by a formula**, not measured from real people or real servers. They are realistic stand-ins: the shapes (and so the lessons) are the ones you will meet in real data, but never quote the figures as facts about the real world.

Create a folder for this chapter (say `da01/`) and save the next block as `datasets.py` inside it. Every later block assumes it can `from datasets import ...`.

```python run
# file: datasets.py
"""Synthetic but realistic practice datasets. Everything is seeded: same numbers every run."""
import math
import random


def incomes(n=200, seed=1):
    """Monthly household income in rupees. Right-skewed (log-normal)."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return [int(round(rng.lognormvariate(math.log(30000), 0.65), -2)) for _ in range(n)]


def house_prices(n=150, seed=2):
    """House prices in lakh rupees. Right-skewed, a few very expensive."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return [round(rng.lognormvariate(math.log(55), 0.5), 1) for _ in range(n)]


def response_times(n=500, seed=3):
    """API response times in milliseconds: mostly fast, about 4% very slow."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    values = []
    for _ in range(n):
        if rng.random() < 0.04:
            values.append(round(rng.uniform(600, 2500), 1))
        else:
            values.append(round(max(20.0, rng.gauss(120, 18)), 1))
    return values


def exam_marks(n=120, seed=4):
    """Marks out of 100, roughly symmetric around 62."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    return [min(100, max(0, round(rng.gauss(62, 12)))) for _ in range(n)]


def two_group_marks(seed=5):
    """Marks of a class with two groups of students: a bimodal shape."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    low = [min(100, max(0, round(rng.gauss(42, 7)))) for _ in range(60)]
    high = [min(100, max(0, round(rng.gauss(78, 7)))) for _ in range(60)]
    return low + high
```

Notes on the code, because it uses a few new-looking things:

- `random.Random(seed)` makes a **private** random generator. Using your own object (instead of the global `random.seed(...)`) means that other code cannot disturb the sequence. Same seed, same numbers, every run and every computer.
- `lognormvariate(mu, sigma)` produces positive, right-skewed numbers: the logarithm of the value is normally distributed. Incomes and prices behave like this in real life.
- `gauss(mu, sigma)` produces the familiar bell shape around `mu`.
- The response-time function mixes two populations: 96% fast requests around 120 ms, 4% very slow ones between 600 and 2500 ms. Real services look like this: a normal path and a rare slow path (a cold cache, a retry, a lock).

### 5.2 First look, always

Never summarise before you have looked. The smallest useful look is: how many, a few values, the smallest and the largest.

```python run
from datasets import incomes, house_prices, response_times, exam_marks, two_group_marks

sets = {
    "incomes (rupees/month)": incomes(),
    "house prices (lakh)": house_prices(),
    "response times (ms)": response_times(),
    "exam marks (/100)": exam_marks(),
    "two-group marks (/100)": two_group_marks(),
}

for name, values in sets.items():
    print(f"{name:24} n={len(values):4}  min={min(values):>9}  max={max(values):>9}  first 5: {values[:5]}")

print()
print("Same seed, same data?", incomes() == incomes())
```

## 6. Kinds of data

### 6.1 Why the kind matters

The first thing you decide about each column is **what kind of thing it is**, because the kind decides which summaries make sense. The average of a column of phone numbers is a number, and it is meaningless.

| Kind | Meaning | Examples | Sensible summaries |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** (categorical) | labels with no order | city, colour, payment method, department | counts, proportions, mode |
| **Ordinal** | labels with a natural order, but the gaps are not equal | star rating, size S/M/L, "agree ... disagree", class rank | median, percentiles, counts per level |
| **Interval** | numbers with equal gaps but no true zero | temperature in Celsius, calendar year | mean, spread, differences (not ratios) |
| **Ratio** | numbers with equal gaps and a true zero | income, height, response time, age | everything, including ratios ("twice as slow") |

You will also hear **discrete** (counted: 0, 1, 2, ... children) versus **continuous** (measured: any value in a range, like 120.4 ms). Marks that are whole numbers are discrete in practice but behave like continuous data with many levels.

### 6.2 Numbers that are not quantities

Some columns look numeric and are not:

- **Identifiers**: roll number, phone number, PIN code, order id. Adding two PIN codes means nothing. Store them as strings.
- **Codes**: `1 = male, 2 = female`; `status 200, 404`. Treat as categories.
- **Dates**: they have a spread and an order, but `statistics.mean` of dates is not the right tool (you will learn date handling in the pandas chapters).

### 6.3 Summarising a categorical column

For categories the summary is a **frequency table**: how many of each, and what fraction. `collections.Counter` does it.

```python run
from collections import Counter

payments = ["UPI", "card", "UPI", "cash", "UPI", "card", "UPI", "UPI", "cash", "wallet",
            "UPI", "card", "UPI", "cash", "UPI", "UPI", "card", "UPI", "wallet", "UPI"]

counts = Counter(payments)
total = len(payments)
print(f"{'method':8} {'count':>5} {'share':>7}")
for method, count in counts.most_common():
    print(f"{method:8} {count:5d} {count / total:7.1%}")

print()
print("mode (most common):", counts.most_common(1)[0][0])
```
```text
method   count   share
UPI         11   55.0%
card         4   20.0%
cash         3   15.0%
wallet       2   10.0%

mode (most common): UPI
```

Percentages need the **total** as the denominator, and a frequency table should always sum to the total. Make a habit of checking `sum(counts.values()) == len(payments)`.

### 6.4 Ordinal data: the average of ranks is suspect

Star ratings are ordinal: the step from 1 to 2 stars is not obviously the same size as from 4 to 5. People still average them, and often get away with it. But the median and the full counts are always honest. Look at two products with the same mean rating:

```python run
import statistics
from collections import Counter

product_a = [3] * 10 + [4] * 10 + [2] * 5                  # nearly everyone is lukewarm
product_b = [5] * 9 + [1] * 7 + [3] * 4                   # people love it or hate it

for name, ratings in [("A", product_a), ("B", product_b)]:
    counts = Counter(ratings)
    table = ", ".join(f"{stars} star: {counts[stars]}" for stars in sorted(counts))
    print(f"product {name}: mean={statistics.mean(ratings):.2f}  median={statistics.median(ratings)}  [{table}]")
```
```text
product A: mean=3.20  median=3  [2 star: 5, 3 star: 10, 4 star: 10]
product B: mean=3.20  median=3.0  [1 star: 7, 3 star: 4, 5 star: 9]
```

The two means are identical (3.20 for both) and even the medians match, but the products are very different: A is mediocre for everybody, B is divisive. **The mean of an ordinal scale hides exactly the thing you need to know.** Show the counts per level. This is a theme of the whole chapter: *one number is never the whole story.*

### 6.5 Interval versus ratio

Twenty degrees Celsius is not "twice as hot" as ten, because zero Celsius is a convention, not "no heat". Differences are fine (20 is 10 degrees warmer than 10); ratios are not. Response time is a **ratio** scale: 200 ms really is twice 100 ms, because 0 ms means no time at all. In practice, you only need to remember: *do not make ratios of interval data, and do not average codes.*

## 7. Reading a CSV safely and cleaning it

### 7.1 Real files are messy

A CSV file ("comma-separated values") is the most common way data reaches you. It looks simple and it is full of traps. Typical problems in real files:

- **Everything is text.** The csv module gives you strings. `"120.5"` is not a number until you convert it.
- **Missing values** written as empty cells, `NA`, `N/A`, `-`, `null`, `?`.
- **Units and symbols** inside cells: `98ms`, `Rs. 1,250`, `45%`.
- **Thousands separators**: `1,204` (inside quotes, so it stays one cell).
- **Impossible values**: negative response times, ages of 400, marks above 100.
- **Duplicates**: the same record twice.
- **Encoding**: a hidden byte-order mark (BOM) at the start of the file from Excel, which glues itself to the first column name.
- **Extra spaces** around values and column names.

The rule: **never delete bad rows silently.** Parse what you can, keep a list of what you rejected and why, and report both. A summary of 487 of 500 rows is fine if you *say* so.

### 7.4 A reusable number parser

Converting a messy cell into a float, or into `None` with a reason, is a small function you will reuse constantly. We put it in its own module, `cleaning.py`, so notebooks and scripts can import it.

```python run
# file: cleaning.py
"""Small, honest cleaning helpers. They never guess silently: they return a reason."""
import csv

MISSING = {"", "na", "n/a", "nan", "null", "none", "-", "?"}


def parse_number(text):
    """Return (value, reason). value is a float or None; reason says why None."""
    cleaned = text.strip().lower()
    if cleaned in MISSING:
        return None, "missing"
    for junk in ("ms", "%", "rs.", "rs", "$"):
        cleaned = cleaned.replace(junk, "")
    cleaned = cleaned.replace(",", "").strip()
    try:
        return float(cleaned), ""
    except ValueError:
        return None, "not a number: " + repr(text)


def load_column(path, column, low=None, high=None, dedupe_on=None):
    """Read one numeric column from a CSV. Returns (values, rejects).

    rejects is a list of (line_number, reason). Nothing is dropped silently.
    """
    values, rejects, seen = [], [], set()
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8-sig") as handle:
        reader = csv.DictReader(handle)
        for line_number, row in enumerate(reader, start=2):   # line 1 is the header
            if dedupe_on is not None:
                key = tuple(row[name] for name in dedupe_on)
                if key in seen:
                    rejects.append((line_number, "duplicate row"))
                    continue
                seen.add(key)
            value, reason = parse_number(row[column])
            if value is None:
                rejects.append((line_number, reason))
            elif low is not None and value < low:
                rejects.append((line_number, f"below {low}: {value}"))
            elif high is not None and value > high:
                rejects.append((line_number, f"above {high}: {value}"))
            else:
                values.append(value)
    return values, rejects
```

Now use it on the whole messy file. The `low=0` rule rejects the negative time (a response time can never be below zero):

```python run
import tempfile

from cleaning import load_column, parse_number

7,/cart,131,200

9,/search,342.7,200
'''

    print(f"  line {line_number}: {reason}")

print("rows kept    :", len(values))
print("rows rejected:", len(rejects))

```
```text

rejected: 5
  line 5: missing
  line 6: missing
  line 7: below 0: -5.0
  line 9: duplicate row
  line 10: not a number: 'abc'

```

Notice that the value `1204.0` was **kept**: it is suspicious (ten times its neighbours) but not impossible, and a suspicious value is not an invalid one. The cleaner only rejects what it can justify; looking at the oddities is a later job (section 13).

The accounting line matters: **kept + rejected must equal the number of data rows.** If it does not, rows vanished, and your summary describes something other than your file.

### 7.5 Judgement calls

Cleaning is a set of decisions, and each should be written down:

- Is a missing value "unknown" or "zero"? An empty salary is not 0.
- Is `1,204` really 1204 ms, or a typing error? (Suspicious values need a look at the source, not a silent fix.)
- Is the negative time a bug in the logger? Then the other values from the same logger may be wrong too.
- Is the duplicate a double-logging bug or two real identical requests? Here the `id` column decides; without an id you cannot tell.

Cleaning decisions change the answer. State them in your report; `load_column` already gives you the list of rejected rows to quote.

### 7.6 What if the file is huge?

For files that do not fit comfortably in memory, loop over the reader without `list(...)`, keeping only the numbers you need, as `load_column` does. For this course's file sizes, a list of floats is perfectly fine: a million floats is around 8 MB of pointers plus the objects.

## 8. The centre: mean, median and mode

### 8.1 Why we want "a typical value"

If someone asks "how long does the bus take?" you do not read out 200 journey times. You say "about 25 minutes". That single number is a **measure of centre** (also: central tendency, or "average"). There is more than one way to find a centre, and they answer slightly different questions:

| Measure | Idea | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **mean** | add up, divide by how many | "If everyone had an equal share, how much would each get?" |
| **median** | the middle value when sorted | "What does the person in the middle have?" |
| **mode** | the most common value | "What happens most often?" |

The word "average" in news and conversation can mean any of the three. A careful writer says which one.

### 8.2 The mean

Add the values, divide by the count: `sum(values) / len(values)`. In symbols, for values x1, x2, ..., xn, the mean is (x1 + x2 + ... + xn) / n.

```python run
import statistics

values = [2, 3, 3, 4, 10]
by_hand = sum(values) / len(values)
print("by hand          :", by_hand)
print("statistics.mean  :", statistics.mean(values))
print("statistics.fmean :", statistics.fmean(values))
```
```text
by hand          : 4.4
statistics.mean  : 4.4
statistics.fmean : 4.4
```

Two functions, same answer here. `statistics.mean` is careful: it keeps exact arithmetic for ints, fractions and decimals. `statistics.fmean` converts to floats and is faster, which matters for big lists. For ordinary analysis use `fmean`; use `mean` when you need the exact type.

The mean has a physical meaning: it is the **balance point**. Imagine each value as a weight on a ruler; the mean is where the ruler balances. Equivalently, the distances to the mean on the left and right cancel out exactly:

```python run
values = [2, 3, 3, 4, 10]
mean = sum(values) / len(values)
deviations = [x - mean for x in values]
print("mean       :", mean)
print("deviations :", deviations)
print("their sum  :", sum(deviations))
```
```text
mean       : 4.4
deviations : [-2.4000000000000004, -1.4000000000000004, -1.4000000000000004, -0.40000000000000036, 5.6]
their sum  : -1.7763568394002505e-15
```

The sum of the deviations is zero. (The printed `-1.78e-15` is not a real remainder: it is floating-point rounding, a number a million million times smaller than the data. On paper the sum is exactly 0.) The big value 10 sits far to the right, so the mean (4.4) is dragged right of where most values (2, 3, 3, 4) cluster: to balance one far weight, the pivot must move toward it.

### 8.3 The median

Sort the values; the median is the middle one. With an even count there are two middle values and the median is their average.

```python run
import statistics


def median(values):
    ordered = sorted(values)            # sorted(), not .sort(): do not touch the caller's list
    n = len(ordered)
    if n == 0:
        raise ValueError("median of an empty list")
    middle = n // 2
    if n % 2 == 1:
        return ordered[middle]
    return (ordered[middle - 1] + ordered[middle]) / 2


odd = [7, 1, 5, 3, 9]
even = [7, 1, 5, 3, 9, 11]
print("odd  :", median(odd), statistics.median(odd))
print("even :", median(even), statistics.median(even))
print("median_low / median_high of the even list:",
      statistics.median_low(even), statistics.median_high(even))
print("original untouched:", odd)
```
```text
odd  : 5 5
even : 6.0 6.0
median_low / median_high of the even list: 5 7
original untouched: [7, 1, 5, 3, 9]
```

Walk through the even case by hand: sorted is `[1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11]`, n is 6, `middle` is 3, so the two middle values are `ordered[2]` = 5 and `ordered[3]` = 7, and the median is 6.0. Notice that 6 is not in the data. If the result must be an actual observed value (for example, a size of shoe), use `median_low` or `median_high`.

The median does not care *how large* the large values are, only that they are above the middle. That is its strength and, as we will see, also its limit.

### 8.4 The mode

The mode is the value that occurs most often. It is the only centre that makes sense for **categories** (the most common payment method), and it works for numbers too.

```python run
import statistics
from collections import Counter

sizes = [38, 40, 40, 41, 42, 42, 42, 43, 44]
print("mode      :", statistics.mode(sizes))
print("counts    :", Counter(sizes).most_common(3))

tie = [1, 1, 2, 2, 3]
print("two modes :", statistics.multimode(tie))
print("mode(tie) :", statistics.mode(tie), "(first one found; since Python 3.8 no error)")

words = ["red", "blue", "red", "green"]
print("mode of words:", statistics.mode(words))
```
```text
mode      : 42
counts    : [(42, 3), (40, 2), (38, 1)]
two modes : [1, 2]
mode(tie) : 1 (first one found; since Python 3.8 no error)
mode of words: red
```

Use `multimode` when ties are possible: it returns a list of *all* the modes. On continuous measurements (response times like 103.4, 115.3, ...) almost every value is unique, so the mode is meaningless. The usual trick is to **bin first**: round the values or group them into intervals and take the most common bin. We do that in section 14.

### 8.5 Mean and median minimise different things

Here is a deeper way to see the difference. For a candidate centre `c`, measure how far the data are from `c`, either as squared distances or as absolute distances. The **mean** is the `c` with the smallest sum of *squared* distances; the **median** is the `c` with the smallest sum of *absolute* distances. Let us check by trying many candidate values on a small list:

```python run
values = [2, 3, 3, 4, 10]
candidates = [x / 10 for x in range(0, 121)]          # 0.0, 0.1, ..., 12.0


def squared(c):
    return sum((x - c) ** 2 for x in values)


def absolute(c):
    return sum(abs(x - c) for x in values)


best_squared = min(candidates, key=squared)
best_absolute = min(candidates, key=absolute)
print("minimises squared distance :", best_squared, "(the mean is 4.4)")
print("minimises absolute distance:", best_absolute, "(the median is 3)")
```
```text
minimises squared distance : 4.4 (the mean is 4.4)
minimises absolute distance: 3.0 (the median is 3)
```

Squaring punishes a far value *very* heavily (a distance of 6 counts 36), so the best compromise moves toward it. Absolute distance counts that same value as only 6, so the best centre stays among the crowd. This is the real reason the mean is "sensitive to outliers" and the median "robust".

### 8.6 What an outlier does

Take a calm list and make one value wild:

```python run
import statistics

calm = [48, 50, 51, 52, 52, 53, 55]
wild = calm[:-1] + [550]               # the last value 55 is mistyped as 550

for name, data in [("calm", calm), ("wild", wild)]:
    print(f"{name}: mean={statistics.fmean(data):7.2f}  median={statistics.median(data):5.1f}  data={data}")
```
```text
calm: mean=  51.57  median= 52.0  data=[48, 50, 51, 52, 52, 53, 55]
wild: mean= 122.29  median= 52.0  data=[48, 50, 51, 52, 52, 53, 550]
```

One mistyped value moved the mean by about 70, and moved the median by 0. This is called **robustness**: the median can survive up to half the data being corrupted before it breaks, the mean can be broken by a single value. Neither is "better" in general; they answer different questions (section 10).

### 8.7 Errors and edge cases

- An empty list has no centre: `statistics.mean([])` raises `StatisticsError`. Check `if not data` first and decide what you want to report.
- `None` values (missing data) must be removed before the call, or you get a `TypeError`. Remove them on purpose and count them.
- `nan` values (from `float("nan")`) poison arithmetic: the mean becomes `nan`, and the median becomes unpredictable because `nan` compares false with everything.

```python run
import statistics

try:
    statistics.mean([])
except statistics.StatisticsError as error:
    print("StatisticsError:", error)

data = [1.0, 2.0, float("nan"), 4.0]
print("sum with nan   :", sum(data))
print("mean with nan  :", statistics.fmean(data))
print("nan == nan     :", float("nan") == float("nan"))
```
```text
StatisticsError: mean requires at least one data point
sum with nan   : nan
mean with nan  : nan
nan == nan     : False
```

```python
def mean_excluding_none(values):
    present = [x for x in values if x is not None]
    if not present:
        raise ValueError("no values to average")
    return sum(present) / len(present), len(values) - len(present)

print(mean_excluding_none([4, None, 8, None, 12]))   # (8.0, 2)
```

Returning the number of ignored values is the important design choice: the caller can see how much was left out, instead of the function silently hiding it.

## 9. Other averages: trimmed, weighted, geometric, harmonic

### 9.1 The trimmed mean: a compromise

Throw away the lowest and highest few percent, then take the mean of the rest. You keep the mean's use of every remaining value while cutting off the extremes. Olympic scoring does this: drop the highest and lowest judge.

```python run
import statistics


def trimmed_mean(values, proportion=0.1):
    """Mean after dropping `proportion` of the data from EACH end."""
    ordered = sorted(values)
    cut = int(len(ordered) * proportion)             # how many to drop per end
    kept = ordered[cut:len(ordered) - cut] if cut else ordered
    return statistics.fmean(kept)


scores = [6.1, 6.3, 6.4, 6.5, 6.5, 6.6, 6.7, 6.8, 6.9, 1.0]     # one judge slipped
print("plain mean   :", round(statistics.fmean(scores), 3))
print("median       :", statistics.median(scores))
print("trimmed 10%  :", round(trimmed_mean(scores, 0.10), 3))
print("trimmed 20%  :", round(trimmed_mean(scores, 0.20), 3))
```
```text
plain mean   : 5.98
median       : 6.5
trimmed 10%  : 6.487
trimmed 20%  : 6.5
```

With 10 scores and a 10% trim, `cut` is 1, so one score is dropped from each end (the 1.0 and the 6.9). The trimmed mean is much closer to what the nine sensible judges said. The price: you have decided, before seeing the data, that the top and bottom 10% are not informative. Report the proportion you used.

### 9.2 The weighted mean

When values do not count equally, weight them. A student's grade where courses have different credits is the classic example.

```python run
import statistics

marks = [80, 60, 90]
credits = [4, 2, 1]

plain = sum(marks) / len(marks)
weighted = sum(m * c for m, c in zip(marks, credits)) / sum(credits)
print("plain mean   :", round(plain, 3))
print("weighted mean:", round(weighted, 3))
print("fmean with weights:", round(statistics.fmean(marks, weights=credits), 3))
```
```text
plain mean   : 76.667
weighted mean: 75.714
fmean with weights: 75.714
```

By hand: (80x4 + 60x2 + 90x1) / (4 + 2 + 1) = (320 + 120 + 90) / 7 = 530 / 7, about 75.714. The plain mean (76.667) overstates the student's result, because the weakest mark carries more credits than the strongest. `statistics.fmean(..., weights=...)` does the same thing.

### 9.3 The geometric mean: for growth and ratios

If a quantity grows by +50% one year and falls by 50% the next, the arithmetic mean of the changes is 0%, and yet you are not back where you started:

```python run
import math
import statistics

growth_factors = [1.50, 0.50]                # +50%, then -50%
start = 100.0
end = start
for factor in growth_factors:
    end *= factor
print("start 100 ->", end, " (a 25% loss, not 0%)")

arithmetic = statistics.fmean(growth_factors)
geometric = statistics.geometric_mean(growth_factors)
print("arithmetic mean factor:", arithmetic, " -> predicts", start * arithmetic ** 2)
print("geometric  mean factor:", round(geometric, 4), " -> predicts", round(start * geometric ** 2, 4))
print("by hand (product ** (1/n)):", round(math.prod(growth_factors) ** (1 / len(growth_factors)), 4))
```
```text
start 100 -> 75.0  (a 25% loss, not 0%)
arithmetic mean factor: 1.0  -> predicts 100.0
geometric  mean factor: 0.866  -> predicts 75.0
by hand (product ** (1/n)): 0.866
```

The **geometric mean** multiplies the values and takes the n-th root. It is the right average for *multiplicative* things: growth factors, price ratios, index numbers. It answers "what constant factor per period would give the same final result?" Here that factor is about 0.866, a loss of 13.4% per year, which compounds to the true 25% loss. The arithmetic mean predicted no loss at all. (It needs positive values only.)

### 9.4 The harmonic mean: for rates

You drive 60 km/h to a town and come back at 40 km/h over the same road. What is your average speed? Not 50. The slower leg takes longer, so you spend more time at 40 than at 60.

```python run
import statistics

distance = 120.0                       # km each way
time_out = distance / 60
time_back = distance / 40
true_average = (2 * distance) / (time_out + time_back)
print("true average speed:", true_average)
print("arithmetic mean   :", statistics.fmean([60, 40]))
print("harmonic mean     :", statistics.harmonic_mean([60, 40]))
```
```text
true average speed: 48.0
arithmetic mean   : 50.0
harmonic mean     : 48.0
```

The **harmonic mean** is the right average of *rates over equal distances (or equal amounts of work)*. Rule of thumb: arithmetic for amounts that add, geometric for factors that multiply, harmonic for rates "per something" averaged over equal "somethings".

| Data | Typical choice |
|---|---|
| symmetric measurements (marks, heights) | mean |
| skewed money or time | median (and percentiles) |
| a few suspect extremes in a long list | trimmed mean, or median |
| items counting unequally | weighted mean |
| growth, returns, ratios | geometric mean |
| speeds, prices per unit, rates over equal amounts | harmonic mean |
| categories | mode |

## 10. When the median beats the mean

### 10.1 The mean is a share, the median is a person

The mean answers: "if the total were split equally, what is each share?" That is the right question for budgets, planning, and anything you will multiply back up (mean cost per item times number of items is the total). The median answers: "what does the middle person get?" That is the right question for "what is typical?"

For symmetric data, the two agree and you can use either. For **skewed** data (a long tail on one side), they separate, and the mean describes nobody in particular. Income is the standard example: a few very high incomes pull the mean up, so most households earn less than the mean.

### 10.2 Worked example 1: household income

```python run
import statistics
from datasets import incomes

data = incomes()
mean = statistics.fmean(data)
median = statistics.median(data)
below_mean = sum(1 for x in data if x < mean)

print(f"n = {len(data)}")
print(f"mean   = {mean:,.0f}")
print(f"median = {median:,.0f}")
print(f"mean / median = {mean / median:.2f}")
print(f"households below the mean: {below_mean} of {len(data)} ({below_mean / len(data):.1%})")
print(f"largest income = {max(data):,}")
```
```text
n = 200
mean   = 36,440
median = 29,950
mean / median = 1.22
households below the mean: 121 of 200 (60.5%)
largest income = 134,000
```

Read it carefully. The mean is clearly higher than the median, and more than half of all households earn less than the "average" income. A headline that says "the average household earns {mean}" would be technically true and would mislead most readers. The median is the number for which exactly half the households earn less and half earn more.

### 10.3 Worked example 2: add one rich household

What does a single very rich household do to each measure?

```python run
import statistics
from datasets import incomes

data = incomes()
bigger = data + [5_000_000]            # one new household enters the data

for label, values in [("before", data), ("after ", bigger)]:
    print(f"{label}: n={len(values)}  mean={statistics.fmean(values):10,.0f}  median={statistics.median(values):8,.0f}")
```
```text
before: n=200  mean=    36,440  median=  29,950
after : n=201  mean=    61,135  median=  30,200
```

One household out of 201 raised the mean by roughly 25,000 rupees per month (from 36,440 to 61,135, a rise of about two-thirds), although the other 200 households did not earn a rupee more. The median moved from 29,950 to 30,200: it can only step to the neighbouring value in the sorted order. If you wanted to know "how well off is the typical household in this town", the median gave you a stable answer and the mean did not.

### 10.4 Worked example 3: house prices

```python run
import statistics
from datasets import house_prices

prices = house_prices()
mean = statistics.fmean(prices)
median = statistics.median(prices)
print(f"mean   = {mean:6.1f} lakh")
print(f"median = {median:6.1f} lakh")

```
```text
mean   =   64.2 lakh
median =   57.6 lakh
mean - median =  6.6 lakh
houses over 100 lakh: 21 of 150
```

The mean (64.2 lakh) is 6.6 lakh above the median (57.6 lakh), because the 21 houses over 100 lakh pull it up. If an agent says "the average house here is 64 lakh", then more than half of all the houses cost less than that; the median says "half the houses cost less than 57.6 lakh and half cost more", which is what a buyer comparing a particular house with the market needs.

### 10.5 Worked example 4: response times

For a server, the picture is different, and the lesson sharper:

```python run
import statistics

times = response_times()

median = statistics.median(times)

print(f"requests            : {len(times)}")
print(f"mean                : {mean:7.1f} ms")
print(f"median              : {median:7.1f} ms")
print(f"requests over 500 ms: {len(slow)} ({len(slow) / len(times):.1%})")
print(f"mean of the others  : {statistics.fmean([t for t in times if t <= 500]):7.1f} ms")

```
```text

median              :   124.9 ms
requests over 500 ms: 24 (4.8%)
mean of the others  :   122.2 ms
mean of the slow ones:  1466.8 ms
```

Four percent of requests were meant to be slow, and by chance 24 of 500 (4.8%) landed above 500 ms. The median describes the experience of a typical request (fast, about 125 ms). Notice what the mean *is* good for here: `mean x number of requests` is the total server time spent, which is what you need for capacity planning. And to describe the unlucky users you need neither: you need a high percentile (section 12).

### 10.6 The rule

Use the **median** when the data are skewed or contain outliers *and* the question is about a typical case. Use the **mean** when the data are roughly symmetric, or when you need a total. When the two differ a lot, **say so**, because the gap itself is information: it is a sign of skew. A quick check:

```python run
import statistics


def skew_hint(values):
    mean, median = statistics.fmean(values), statistics.median(values)
    spread = statistics.pstdev(values)
    gap = (mean - median) / spread if spread else 0.0
    if gap > 0.1:
        return gap, "mean above median: right-skewed (tail to the high side)"
    if gap < -0.1:
        return gap, "mean below median: left-skewed (tail to the low side)"
    return gap, "mean and median close: roughly symmetric"


from datasets import incomes, house_prices, response_times, exam_marks

for name, data in [("incomes", incomes()), ("house prices", house_prices()),
                   ("response times", response_times()), ("exam marks", exam_marks())]:
    gap, text = skew_hint(data)
    print(f"{name:15} (mean - median)/stdev = {gap:6.3f}  {text}")
```
```text
incomes         (mean - median)/stdev =  0.267  mean above median: right-skewed (tail to the high side)
house prices    (mean - median)/stdev =  0.217  mean above median: right-skewed (tail to the high side)
response times  (mean - median)/stdev =  0.196  mean above median: right-skewed (tail to the high side)
exam marks      (mean - median)/stdev = -0.007  mean and median close: roughly symmetric
```

The quantity `(mean - median) / standard deviation` is a rough skew indicator (it is Pearson's second skewness coefficient, up to a factor of 3). A value near zero suggests symmetry; clearly positive means a tail to the right. The threshold 0.1 is a rule of thumb, not a law. We will compute better skew numbers in section 14.

## 11. Spread: range, variance, standard deviation, IQR, MAD

### 11.1 A centre is not enough

Two classes both have a mean mark of 60.

```python run
import statistics

class_a = [58, 59, 60, 60, 60, 61, 62]
class_b = [20, 35, 50, 60, 70, 85, 100]

for name, marks in [("A", class_a), ("B", class_b)]:
    print(f"class {name}: mean={statistics.fmean(marks):.1f}  median={statistics.median(marks)}  marks={marks}")
```
```text
class A: mean=60.0  median=60  marks=[58, 59, 60, 60, 60, 61, 62]
class B: mean=60.0  median=60  marks=[20, 35, 50, 60, 70, 85, 100]
```

Same mean, same median, completely different classes: in A everybody is close to 60, in B somebody has failed and somebody has 100. The missing information is the **spread**: how far values typically lie from the centre. A good summary always has a centre and a spread.

### 11.2 Range

The simplest spread: `max - min`. It is easy to understand and it uses only two values, so one wild value ruins it.

```python run
for name, marks in [("A", [58, 59, 60, 60, 60, 61, 62]), ("B", [20, 35, 50, 60, 70, 85, 100])]:
    print(f"class {name}: range = {max(marks)} - {min(marks)} = {max(marks) - min(marks)}")
```
```text
class A: range = 62 - 58 = 4
class B: range = 100 - 20 = 80
```

### 11.3 Variance and standard deviation

We want one number for "typical distance from the mean". The natural idea is the average of the deviations, but that is always zero (section 8.2). So we **square** each deviation first (so negatives cannot cancel), then average them. That average is the **variance**. Its unit is the square of the data's unit (rupees squared, marks squared), which is awkward, so we take the square root to get back to the original unit: the **standard deviation** (sd, or sigma).

```python run
import math
import statistics

marks = [20, 35, 50, 60, 70, 85, 100]
n = len(marks)
mean = sum(marks) / n

print("deviations        :", [round(x - mean, 2) for x in marks])
squares = [(x - mean) ** 2 for x in marks]
print("squared deviations:", [round(s, 1) for s in squares])
print("sum of squares    :", round(sum(squares), 1))
print()
print("population variance (divide by n)  :", round(sum(squares) / n, 2))
print("sample variance     (divide by n-1):", round(sum(squares) / (n - 1), 2))
print("pvariance / variance from statistics:", round(statistics.pvariance(marks), 2), round(statistics.variance(marks), 2))
print("pstdev / stdev:", round(statistics.pstdev(marks), 2), round(statistics.stdev(marks), 2))
print("sqrt of sample variance:", round(math.sqrt(sum(squares) / (n - 1)), 2))
```
```text
deviations        : [-40.0, -25.0, -10.0, 0.0, 10.0, 25.0, 40.0]
squared deviations: [1600.0, 625.0, 100.0, 0.0, 100.0, 625.0, 1600.0]
sum of squares    : 4650.0

population variance (divide by n)  : 664.29
sample variance     (divide by n-1): 775.0
pvariance / variance from statistics: 664.29 775
pstdev / stdev: 25.77 27.84
sqrt of sample variance: 27.84
```

There are two versions, and the difference is the single most confused point in introductory statistics:

- **Population** variance divides by `n`. Use it when your data *is* the whole population you care about (all 7 students in this class, and you are not trying to say anything about other classes).
- **Sample** variance divides by `n - 1`. Use it when your data is a *sample* from a larger population and you want to estimate that population's variance. The `n - 1` is called **Bessel's correction**.

In `statistics`: `pvariance` / `pstdev` are population ("p"), `variance` / `stdev` are sample. **The names without a "p" are the sample versions**, which is the opposite of what some people expect.

### 11.4 Why `n - 1`? An experiment

The deviations are measured from the **sample's own mean**, and a sample's mean sits closer to its own values than the true population mean does (it is chosen to be the best fit). So squared deviations from the sample mean come out a little too small on average. Dividing by `n - 1` instead of `n` makes up for it exactly. We can *see* this by simulation: create a population with a known variance, draw many small samples, and compare the average of the two estimates with the truth.

```python run
import random
import statistics

rng = random.Random(7)
population_sd = 10.0                 # the truth: variance is exactly 100
trials = 20000
sample_size = 5

sum_n = 0.0
sum_n_minus_1 = 0.0
for _ in range(trials):
    sample = [rng.gauss(50, population_sd) for _ in range(sample_size)]
    sum_n += statistics.pvariance(sample)            # divide by n
    sum_n_minus_1 += statistics.variance(sample)     # divide by n - 1

print("true variance                    :", population_sd ** 2)
print("average estimate, divide by n    :", round(sum_n / trials, 1))
print("average estimate, divide by n - 1:", round(sum_n_minus_1 / trials, 1))
print("expected for divide by n: 100 * (n-1)/n =", 100 * (sample_size - 1) / sample_size)
```
```text
true variance                    : 100.0
average estimate, divide by n    : 80.0
average estimate, divide by n - 1: 100.0
expected for divide by n: 100 * (n-1)/n = 80.0
```

Dividing by `n` underestimates, by almost exactly the factor `(n - 1) / n` (80 for samples of 5). Dividing by `n - 1` lands close to the truth of 100 (the small difference is random noise from 20,000 samples). For large `n` the correction is tiny; for small `n` it matters a lot.

### 11.5 Reading a standard deviation

A rough picture: most values lie within about one standard deviation of the mean, and almost all within three, **when the data are roughly bell-shaped**. For skewed data the mean and sd are less informative, as we will see. For class B above, the sample sd is 27.84 marks: B's marks typically differ from 60 by roughly 28 marks; for class A, the sd is 1.29.

```python run
import statistics

for name, marks in [("A", [58, 59, 60, 60, 60, 61, 62]), ("B", [20, 35, 50, 60, 70, 85, 100])]:
    mean, sd = statistics.fmean(marks), statistics.stdev(marks)
    inside = sum(1 for x in marks if abs(x - mean) <= sd)
    print(f"class {name}: mean={mean:.1f}  sd={sd:.2f}  within one sd: {inside} of {len(marks)}")
```
```text
class A: mean=60.0  sd=1.29  within one sd: 5 of 7
class B: mean=60.0  sd=27.84  within one sd: 5 of 7
```

### 11.6 Spread measures that resist outliers

Variance squares the deviations, so a far value has an enormous effect on it, just as it has on the mean. Two spread measures use ranks and so resist outliers:

- **IQR** (interquartile range): the distance between the 75th and 25th percentile. It describes the width of the middle half of the data. (Percentiles are defined carefully in section 12; here we use `statistics.quantiles`.)
- **MAD** (median absolute deviation): the median of `|x - median|`. It is "the median distance from the median".

```python run
import statistics


def iqr(values):
    q1, _, q3 = statistics.quantiles(values, n=4, method="inclusive")
    return q3 - q1


def mad(values):
    centre = statistics.median(values)
    return statistics.median(abs(x - centre) for x in values)


calm = [48, 50, 51, 52, 52, 53, 55]
wild = calm[:-1] + [550]

print(f"{'':6} {'range':>7} {'stdev':>8} {'IQR':>6} {'MAD':>6}")
for name, data in [("calm", calm), ("wild", wild)]:
    print(f"{name:6} {max(data) - min(data):7} {statistics.stdev(data):8.2f} {iqr(data):6.1f} {mad(data):6.1f}")
```
```text
         range    stdev    IQR    MAD
calm         7     2.23    2.0    1.0
wild       502   188.61    2.0    1.0
```

One mistyped value multiplied the range by about 70 (7 to 502) and the standard deviation by about 85 (2.23 to 188.61), but the IQR (2.0) and the MAD (1.0) did not change at all. Robust spreads are the right choice for the same data where the median beats the mean.

### 11.7 Coefficient of variation: spread relative to size

Is a standard deviation of 10 big? For heights in centimetres (mean 170) it is moderate; for marks with mean 30 it is large. The **coefficient of variation** (CV) is `stdev / mean`, often shown as a percentage. It lets you compare the spread of things measured on different scales, such as the consistency of a price in rupees and a weight in kilograms. It only makes sense for **ratio** data with a mean well above zero.

```python run
import statistics
from datasets import incomes, house_prices, response_times, exam_marks

print(f"{'dataset':16} {'mean':>10} {'stdev':>10} {'CV':>7} {'IQR':>9} {'MAD':>9}")
for name, data in [("incomes", incomes()), ("house prices", house_prices()),
                   ("response times", response_times()), ("exam marks", exam_marks())]:
    mean, sd = statistics.fmean(data), statistics.stdev(data)
    q1, _, q3 = statistics.quantiles(data, n=4, method="inclusive")
    centre = statistics.median(data)
    mad = statistics.median(abs(x - centre) for x in data)
    print(f"{name:16} {mean:10.1f} {sd:10.1f} {sd / mean:7.2f} {q3 - q1:9.1f} {mad:9.1f}")
```
```text
dataset                mean      stdev      CV       IQR       MAD
incomes             36440.5    24359.0    0.67   35450.0   14150.0
house prices           64.2       30.7    0.48      36.6      19.1
response times        186.7      315.3    1.69      25.3      13.2
exam marks             62.9       11.3    0.18      13.5       7.0
```

Compare the response times row with the exam marks row. The marks have a CV of 0.18. The response times have a CV of 1.69: the standard deviation (315.3 ms) is bigger than the mean (186.7 ms) and more than double the typical response (the median is 124.9 ms), because the slow requests drive it. The IQR (25.3 ms) and the MAD (13.2 ms), which ignore the slow tail, describe how consistent the *typical* requests are. Two different questions, two different answers; neither is wrong.

### 11.8 Which spread?

| Spread | Uses | Resists outliers? | Use it when |
|---|---|---|---|
| range | min and max | no | a quick look, or the extremes are the point |
| variance / sd | every value | no | roughly symmetric data, and it feeds later formulas |
| IQR | middle half | yes | skewed data, or with the median |
| MAD | every value, via ranks | yes | robust outlier detection |
| CV | sd / mean | no | comparing different scales (ratio data) |

A pairing to remember: **mean with standard deviation; median with IQR (or MAD).** Mixing them (a median with a standard deviation) is not an error, but it hides intent.


Remember that `stdev` and `quantiles` both need at least two values; a real function should check `len(values)` first.

## 12. Percentiles and quantiles

### 12.1 What a percentile is

The **p-th percentile** is a value below which about p percent of the data fall. The 50th percentile is the median. The 25th, 50th and 75th are the **quartiles** (Q1, Q2, Q3). The 10th, 20th, ..., 90th are **deciles**. All of them are **quantiles**: a quantile is a percentile expressed as a fraction (the 0.9 quantile is the 90th percentile).

Percentiles are everywhere:

- "Your child is in the 90th percentile for height" (taller than about 90% of children of that age).
- "p99 latency is 800 ms" (99 out of 100 requests finished in 800 ms or less; the slowest 1% took longer).
- Entrance exams report percentile *ranks* (what percent of candidates scored below you).

### 12.2 There is no single definition

Here is a surprise: ask "what is the 25th percentile of `[15, 20, 35, 40, 50]`?" and different software gives different answers. The data has 5 values; 25% of the way through is somewhere between the 1st and 2nd value (15 and 20), and **how you interpolate is a convention**. The common conventions are:

1. **Nearest rank**: pick an actual data value. The p-th percentile is the value at (1-based) position `ceil(p/100 * n)` in the sorted list.
2. **Linear interpolation, "inclusive"**: treat the smallest value as the 0th percentile and the largest as the 100th; position = `(n - 1) * p/100` (0-based), and interpolate between the two neighbours. This is Excel's `PERCENTILE.INC`, and the default in NumPy.
3. **Linear interpolation, "exclusive"**: position = `(n + 1) * p/100` (1-based). The extremes are *not* the 0th and 100th percentile, because a sample probably does not contain the true minimum and maximum. This is Excel's `PERCENTILE.EXC`. For percentiles too close to 0 or 100 on a small sample, Excel reports an error, while Python's `statistics.quantiles` extrapolates beyond the data.

Python's `statistics.quantiles(data, n=..., method=...)` supports the two interpolation conventions (`"exclusive"` is its default; `"inclusive"` is the other). Let us write all three by hand and compare them with the library.

```python run
# file: percentiles.py
"""Three ways to compute a percentile, written by hand."""
import math


def percentile_nearest_rank(values, p):
    """Smallest data value with at least p percent of the data at or below it."""
    ordered = sorted(values)
    rank = max(1, math.ceil(p / 100 * len(ordered)))      # 1-based position
    return ordered[rank - 1]


def percentile_inclusive(values, p):
    """Linear interpolation, first and last values are the 0th and 100th percentile."""
    ordered = sorted(values)
    position = (len(ordered) - 1) * p / 100                # 0-based, may be fractional
    low = math.floor(position)
    high = min(low + 1, len(ordered) - 1)
    fraction = position - low
    return ordered[low] + (ordered[high] - ordered[low]) * fraction


def percentile_exclusive(values, p):
    """Linear interpolation on positions (n + 1) * p / 100, counted from 1."""
    ordered = sorted(values)
    n = len(ordered)
    position = (n + 1) * p / 100                           # 1-based
    if position < 1 or position > n:
        raise ValueError(f"p={p} is too extreme for {n} values with the exclusive method")
    low = math.floor(position)
    high = min(low + 1, n)
    fraction = position - low
    return ordered[low - 1] + (ordered[high - 1] - ordered[low - 1]) * fraction
```

Now compare them with each other and with `statistics.quantiles`. For the library, the percentile `p` is the `p`-th of the 99 cut points that `n=100` returns.

```python run
import statistics
from percentiles import percentile_nearest_rank, percentile_inclusive, percentile_exclusive

data = [15, 20, 35, 40, 50]
inc = statistics.quantiles(data, n=100, method="inclusive")
exc = statistics.quantiles(data, n=100, method="exclusive")

print(f"{'p':>3} | {'nearest':>7} | {'incl(mine)':>10} {'incl(lib)':>9} | {'excl(mine)':>10} {'excl(lib)':>9}")
for p in [25, 50, 75, 90]:
    try:
        mine_exc = f"{percentile_exclusive(data, p):10.2f}"
    except ValueError:
        mine_exc = "   refused"
    print(f"{p:3d} | {percentile_nearest_rank(data, p):7} | {percentile_inclusive(data, p):10.2f} {inc[p - 1]:9.2f} | {mine_exc} {exc[p - 1]:9.2f}")
```
```text
  p | nearest | incl(mine) incl(lib) | excl(mine) excl(lib)
 25 |      20 |      20.00     20.00 |      17.50     17.50
 50 |      35 |      35.00     35.00 |      35.00     35.00
 75 |      40 |      40.00     40.00 |      45.00     45.00
 90 |      50 |      46.00     46.00 |    refused     54.00
```

Work one value out by hand. The 25th percentile, five values:

- Nearest rank: `ceil(0.25 x 5)` = `ceil(1.25)` = 2, so the 2nd value, **20**.
- Inclusive: position `(5 - 1) x 0.25` = 1.0 (0-based), which is exactly the 2nd value again, **20**.
- Exclusive: position `(5 + 1) x 0.25` = 1.5 (1-based), halfway between the 1st (15) and 2nd (20) value: **17.5**.

So for this small list the 25th percentile is 17.5 or 20 depending on the convention. The 90th percentile shows the danger of the exclusive method on tiny samples: position `6 x 0.9` = 5.4 is beyond the 5th value. The library **extrapolates** and returns 54, which is larger than every value in the data; my version refuses and says why. In the table, my versions and the library agree wherever mine gives an answer.

For realistic sizes the methods agree closely. They are only visibly different for small samples and extreme percentiles. The rule: **when you report a percentile, say which method you used**, and use the same method every time you compare.

### 12.3 Sanity checks for any percentile function

- The 0th percentile is the min and the 100th is the max (inclusive methods only).
- The 50th percentile is the median.
- It is **monotonic**: a larger p never gives a smaller value.
- It never leaves the range `[min, max]` of the data (true for nearest rank and the inclusive method; the exclusive method can step outside, as the 54 above showed).

```python run

from datasets import exam_marks

print("p0 / p100          :", percentile_inclusive(data, 0), percentile_inclusive(data, 100), " min/max:", min(data), max(data))
values = [percentile_inclusive(data, p) for p in range(0, 101, 5)]
print("monotonic          :", values == sorted(values))
print("nearest rank is a real data value:", percentile_nearest_rank(data, 37) in data)
```

This is the standard way to describe latency: **p50** (the typical request), **p95** and **p99** (the bad days), and the maximum. How many values define a percentile? With 500 values, p99 is determined by the slowest five. With 20 values, p99 is just the maximum (and the exclusive method may return a value beyond the data). **Do not report tail percentiles from small samples.**

### 12.5 The percentile rank: the other direction

"What percentile is a score of 75?" is the inverse question: what fraction of the data lies below this value?

```python run
from datasets import exam_marks


def percentile_rank(values, score):
    """Percent of values strictly below `score`, plus half of those equal to it."""
    below = sum(1 for x in values if x < score)
    equal = sum(1 for x in values if x == score)
    return 100 * (below + 0.5 * equal) / len(values)


marks = exam_marks()
for score in [40, 62, 75, 90]:
    print(f"score {score}: percentile rank {percentile_rank(marks, score):5.1f}")
```
```text

score 62: percentile rank  45.0
score 75: percentile rank  85.4
score 90: percentile rank  99.2
```

Counting half the ties is a common convention (the "mean percentile rank"), and it keeps the rank of the median at about 50. Other sources count ties entirely below or entirely above. Again: say which.

## 13. Five-number summary, box plots, outliers and z-scores

### 13.1 The five-number summary

Five values summarise a dataset's centre, spread and extremes with no assumption about its shape: **minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum.**

```python run
for name, data in [("exam marks", exam_marks()), ("response times", response_times())]:

```

### 13.2 The box plot in plain text

A **box plot** draws those five numbers: a box from Q1 to Q3 with a mark at the median, "whiskers" out to the most extreme *ordinary* points, and dots for outliers beyond. We can draw it with characters.

```python run
# file: textplots.py
"""Plain-text charts: box plot and histogram."""
import statistics


def fences(values, k=1.5):
    q1, _, q3 = statistics.quantiles(values, n=4, method="inclusive")
    spread = q3 - q1
    return q1 - k * spread, q3 + k * spread


def box_plot_line(values, lo, hi, width=60):
    """One text row. The scale runs from `lo` to `hi` over `width` characters."""
    q1, med, q3 = statistics.quantiles(values, n=4, method="inclusive")
    low_fence, high_fence = fences(values)
    inside = [x for x in values if low_fence <= x <= high_fence]
    whisker_lo, whisker_hi = min(inside), max(inside)

    def col(x):
        return min(width - 1, max(0, round((x - lo) / (hi - lo) * (width - 1))))

    row = [" "] * width
    for c in range(col(whisker_lo), col(whisker_hi) + 1):
        row[c] = "-"
    for c in range(col(q1), col(q3) + 1):
        row[c] = "="
    row[col(whisker_lo)] = "|"
    row[col(whisker_hi)] = "|"
    row[col(med)] = "#"
    for x in values:
        if x < low_fence or x > high_fence:
            row[col(x)] = "o"
    return "".join(row)


def histogram(values, bins=10, width=40, low=None, high=None):
    """Return text lines: one row per bin, bar length proportional to the count."""
    low = min(values) if low is None else low
    high = max(values) if high is None else high
    step = (high - low) / bins
    counts = [0] * bins
    for x in values:
        index = min(bins - 1, int((x - low) / step))      # the maximum goes into the last bin
        counts[index] += 1
    biggest = max(counts)
    lines = []
    for i, count in enumerate(counts):
        left, right = low + i * step, low + (i + 1) * step
        bar = "#" * round(count / biggest * width)
        lines.append(f"{left:8.1f} to {right:8.1f} | {bar} {count}")
    return lines
```

```python run

from datasets import exam_marks, two_group_marks, response_times

print("exam marks, scale 0 to 100")

print("0" + " " * 28 + "50" + " " * 27 + "100")

```

How to read it: `|` marks the end of a whisker, `=` is the box (Q1 to Q3), `#` is the median, `o` marks a value beyond the fences. ### 13.3 The 1.5 x IQR rule for outliers

John Tukey's rule for the box plot: a value is flagged as an outlier if it lies below `Q1 - 1.5 x IQR` or above `Q3 + 1.5 x IQR`. These two limits are the **fences**. The factor 1.5 is a convention. For perfectly bell-shaped data the rule flags about 0.7% of values, so a few flags in a large normal sample are not a surprise.

Think of the rule as "this value deserves a look", not "this value is wrong". Real systems have real extremes (the one customer who is truly rich; the day the server was really down).

```python run
import statistics
from textplots import fences
from datasets import incomes, house_prices, response_times, exam_marks

print(f"{'dataset':15} {'low fence':>10} {'high fence':>11} {'low':>4} {'high':>5}  share flagged")
for name, data in [("exam marks", exam_marks()), ("incomes", incomes()),
                   ("house prices", house_prices()), ("response times", response_times())]:
    lo, hi = fences(data)
    n_low = sum(1 for x in data if x < lo)
    n_high = sum(1 for x in data if x > hi)
    print(f"{name:15} {lo:10.1f} {hi:11.1f} {n_low:4d} {n_high:5d}  {(n_low + n_high) / len(data):.1%}")
```
```text
dataset          low fence  high fence  low  high  share flagged
exam marks            35.5        89.5    1     1  1.7%
incomes           -35600.0    106200.0    0     4  2.0%
house prices         -11.1       135.3    0     4  2.7%
response times        72.8       174.1    1    25  5.2%
```

Notice that for right-skewed data (incomes, prices) the flagged values are on the high side only, and that the lower fence for incomes is **negative** (-35,600): no household can earn that, so the rule's lower half is meaningless here. That is the skew showing itself again: the box plot rule is mildly unfair to skewed data, because a genuinely ordinary long tail gets flagged. For these data a log transform before drawing helps (you will see why in the pandas chapters).

### 13.4 What to do with an outlier

1. **Check for an error**: a typo, a unit mix-up, a sensor fault. Trace the value back to its source. If it is an error, fix or remove it, and write down that you did.
2. **If it is real, keep it.** Removing true extremes because they are inconvenient is how analyses lie.
3. **Report both ways** when it matters: "mean 186.7 ms; 122.2 ms without the 24 requests over 500 ms". Or use robust measures (median, IQR) that do not care.
4. **Never delete silently.**

### 13.5 Z-scores

A **z-score** says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean: `z = (x - mean) / sd`. It converts any value into a unit-free number so you can compare across scales: "a mark of 80 in a class with mean 62 and sd 12 is z = 1.5; a mark of 90 in a class with mean 70 and sd 20 is z = 1.0, so the first student did relatively better."

```python run
import statistics
from datasets import exam_marks


def z_scores(values):
    mean, sd = statistics.fmean(values), statistics.stdev(values)
    return [(x - mean) / sd for x in values]


marks = exam_marks()
zs = z_scores(marks)
print("mean of z-scores :", round(statistics.fmean(zs), 10))
print("stdev of z-scores:", round(statistics.stdev(zs), 10))
print("largest |z|      :", round(max(abs(z) for z in zs), 2), " (mark", marks[[abs(z) for z in zs].index(max(abs(z) for z in zs))], ")")
for k in (1, 2, 3):
    share = sum(1 for z in zs if abs(z) <= k) / len(zs)
    print(f"share within {k} sd: {share:.1%}")
```
```text
mean of z-scores : 0.0
stdev of z-scores: 1.0
largest |z|      : 3.29  (mark 100 )
share within 1 sd: 69.2%
share within 2 sd: 94.2%
share within 3 sd: 99.2%
```

Standardised data always has mean 0 and standard deviation 1; that is a quick check that your z-score code is right. For bell-shaped data about 68% of values lie within 1 sd, 95% within 2 and 99.7% within 3 (the "68-95-99.7 rule"), and the marks follow it closely.

A common outlier rule is "flag `|z| > 3`". It has a weakness: the outlier inflates the mean and, even more, the standard deviation, which makes its own z-score smaller. This is called **masking**.

```python run
import statistics

data = [50, 52, 49, 51, 48, 50, 53, 47, 51, 52] + [95]              # one obvious outlier
mean, sd = statistics.fmean(data), statistics.stdev(data)
print(f"mean={mean:.2f} sd={sd:.2f}  z of 95 = {(95 - mean) / sd:.2f}")

data2 = [50, 52, 49, 51, 48, 50, 53, 47, 51, 52] + [95, 98, 100]    # three obvious outliers
mean, sd = statistics.fmean(data2), statistics.stdev(data2)
print(f"mean={mean:.2f} sd={sd:.2f}  z of 95 = {(95 - mean) / sd:.2f}   z of 100 = {(100 - mean) / sd:.2f}")

centre = statistics.median(data2)
mad = statistics.median(abs(x - centre) for x in data2)
print(f"median={centre} MAD={mad}")
print("robust (modified) z of 95 :", round(0.6745 * (95 - centre) / mad, 1))
print("robust (modified) z of 100:", round(0.6745 * (100 - centre) / mad, 1))
```
```text
mean=54.36 sd=13.60  z of 95 = 2.99
mean=61.23 sd=20.86  z of 95 = 1.62   z of 100 = 1.86
median=51 MAD=2
robust (modified) z of 95 : 14.8
robust (modified) z of 100: 16.5
```

A single outlier of 95 among values near 50 gets a z-score of 2.99, just under the "3" line. With three outliers (95, 98, 100) the plain z-scores shrink to 1.62 and 1.86, nowhere near 3, although those values are far from the other ten. The **modified z-score**, based on the median (51) and the MAD (2), gives 14.8 and 16.5 instead. The rule of thumb for the modified z-score is to flag `> 3.5`. The constant 0.6745 rescales the MAD to be comparable with a standard deviation for bell-shaped data.

The three in words:

- **Uniform**: bars about equally tall. No "typical" value; centre statistics say little.
- **Right-skewed** (also called positively skewed): a tall bar at the low end and a tail stretching to the high end. Incomes, house prices, waiting times, file sizes. Mean above median.
- **Left-skewed**: the mirror image. Scores on an easy exam (most near the top, a few very low), age at retirement. Mean below median.

### 14.3 Shape and the mean-median gap

| Shape | Tail | Mean vs median | Better centre | Better spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| symmetric (bell) | both | about equal | mean (median also fine) | sd |
| right-skewed | high | mean > median | median | IQR / MAD |
| left-skewed | low | mean < median | median | IQR / MAD |
| bimodal | two clusters | mean in the valley | none: split the groups | per group |
| uniform | none | about equal | either (little meaning) | range / sd |
| heavy-tailed | extreme values | mean unstable | median, trimmed mean | IQR / percentiles |

| Rule | Number of bins | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| square root | `ceil(sqrt(n))` | quick default |
| Sturges | `ceil(log2(n)) + 1` | fine for small, bell-like data |
| Freedman-Diaconis | bin width `2 x IQR / n^(1/3)` | uses the IQR, so it copes with outliers and skew |

```python run
import math
import statistics
from datasets import incomes, exam_marks, response_times


def bin_suggestions(values):
    n = len(values)
    q1, _, q3 = statistics.quantiles(values, n=4, method="inclusive")
    width = 2 * (q3 - q1) / n ** (1 / 3)
    span = max(values) - min(values)
    return {
        "sqrt": math.ceil(math.sqrt(n)),
        "sturges": math.ceil(math.log2(n)) + 1,
        "fd_width": round(width, 1),
        "fd_bins": math.ceil(span / width) if width else None,
    }


for name, data in [("exam marks", exam_marks()), ("incomes", incomes()), ("response times", response_times())]:
    print(f"{name:15} n={len(data):4}  {bin_suggestions(data)}")
```

```text
incomes         n= 200  {'sqrt': 15, 'sturges': 9, 'fd_width': 12123.7, 'fd_bins': 11}
response times  n= 500  {'sqrt': 23, 'sturges': 10, 'fd_width': 6.4, 'fd_bins': 371}
```

For the response times, Freedman-Diaconis wants a very narrow bin (the IQR is only 25 ms), which, applied over the whole range up to 2,436 ms, gives hundreds of bins. This is the heavy tail again. The practical solution is what we did in 14.2: zoom to the bulk, count the tail separately, and say so. Rules of thumb are starting points; always try two or three bin counts and look.

### 14.5 Skewness: a number for the shape

Skewness measures asymmetry in one number. The population version is the mean of the **cubed** standardised deviations: `skew = mean(((x - mean) / sd) ** 3)`. Cubing keeps the sign (so left and right differ) and magnifies big deviations (so a long tail dominates). Positive means a right tail, negative a left tail, near 0 symmetric.

```python run
import statistics
import random
from datasets import incomes, house_prices, response_times, exam_marks, two_group_marks


def skewness(values):
    """Population skewness: mean of cubed z-scores (z uses the population sd)."""
    mean = statistics.fmean(values)
    sd = statistics.pstdev(values)
    return statistics.fmean(((x - mean) / sd) ** 3 for x in values)


def excess_kurtosis(values):
    """Mean of z ** 4, minus 3. Positive: heavier tails than a bell curve."""
    mean = statistics.fmean(values)
    sd = statistics.pstdev(values)
    return statistics.fmean(((x - mean) / sd) ** 4 for x in values) - 3


rng = random.Random(5)
bell = [rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(5000)]
mirrored = [-x for x in incomes()]                    # flip incomes to see the sign change

print(f"{'dataset':22} {'skewness':>9} {'excess kurtosis':>16}")
for name, data in [("normal, 5000 draws", bell), ("exam marks", exam_marks()),
                   ("two-group marks", two_group_marks()), ("house prices", house_prices()),
                   ("incomes", incomes()), ("incomes, flipped", mirrored),
                   ("response times", response_times())]:
    print(f"{name:22} {skewness(data):9.2f} {excess_kurtosis(data):16.2f}")
```
```text
dataset                 skewness  excess kurtosis
normal, 5000 draws          0.01             0.01
exam marks                  0.12             0.52
two-group marks             0.06            -1.46
house prices                0.99             0.89
incomes                     1.31             2.05
incomes, flipped           -1.31             2.05
response times              5.28            28.50
```

How to read it: values within about +-0.5 are roughly symmetric; beyond +-1 clearly skewed. The normal draws (0.01) and the exam marks (0.12) are symmetric, the house prices (0.99) and incomes (1.31) are right-skewed, and the response times (5.28) are extremely so. The flipped incomes show the same size and the opposite sign, which proves that the sign tells the direction. The two-group marks have skewness near 0 (0.06) even though they are very much *not* a bell: a single number cannot see two humps (always look at the histogram). **Kurtosis** measures tail weight: 0 for a bell curve (that is why we subtract 3), positive for heavy tails, negative for light tails and flat shapes (the two-group marks, -1.46). The response times, with a few extreme values, have a very large positive kurtosis (28.50).

There is also a "sample" version of skewness with a small-sample correction; for hundreds of values the two are close, and we stay with the simple form.

## 15. Summaries that lie

A summary is a compression, and compression loses information. Sometimes the lost information would have changed the conclusion. Four ways it happens.

### 15.1 Simpson's paradox: the groups disagree with the total

A famous medical comparison (from a 1986 study of two treatments for kidney stones; the counts are used here only as arithmetic) has these counts of successes out of patients:

| | Small stones | Large stones | All |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treatment A | 81 of 87 | 192 of 263 | 273 of 350 |
| Treatment B | 234 of 270 | 55 of 80 | 289 of 350 |

```python run
a = {"small": (81, 87), "large": (192, 263)}
b = {"small": (234, 270), "large": (55, 80)}


def rate(successes, total):
    return successes / total


for group in ("small", "large"):
    print(f"{group:6}: A = {rate(*a[group]):.1%}   B = {rate(*b[group]):.1%}   better: {'A' if rate(*a[group]) > rate(*b[group]) else 'B'}")

total_a = (sum(s for s, t in a.values()), sum(t for s, t in a.values()))
total_b = (sum(s for s, t in b.values()), sum(t for s, t in b.values()))
print(f"overall: A = {rate(*total_a):.1%} ({total_a[0]} of {total_a[1]})   B = {rate(*total_b):.1%} ({total_b[0]} of {total_b[1]})")

print()
print("share of A's patients with large stones:", f"{a['large'][1] / total_a[1]:.0%}")
print("share of B's patients with large stones:", f"{b['large'][1] / total_b[1]:.0%}")
```
```text
small : A = 93.1%   B = 86.7%   better: A
large : A = 73.0%   B = 68.8%   better: A
overall: A = 78.0% (273 of 350)   B = 82.6% (289 of 350)

share of A's patients with large stones: 75%
share of B's patients with large stones: 23%
```

Treatment A is better for small stones **and** better for large stones, yet B looks better overall. The reason is in the last two lines: A was given mostly to the hard cases (75% large stones) and B mostly to the easy cases (23%). The overall rate mixes the group rates using different weights. This is **Simpson's paradox**. The lesson is not "ignore totals" but: *before trusting a comparison of overall averages, check whether the groups being compared are made of the same kinds of cases.* Always ask: is there a hidden variable that splits the data into groups of different difficulty, size or type?

### 15.2 Same summary, different data

Three datasets can have the same mean and standard deviation and be completely different. We generate three shapes (seeded), then rescale each to have mean exactly 50 and standard deviation exactly 10:

### 15.3 Averaging averages

You cannot average group averages without weighting by group size.

```python run
schools = {
    "School A": (30, 80.0),            # (students, mean mark)
    "School B": (300, 60.0),
}
plain = sum(m for n, m in schools.values()) / len(schools)
weighted = sum(n * m for n, m in schools.values()) / sum(n for n, m in schools.values())
print("average of the two averages:", plain)
print("true mean of all 330 students:", round(weighted, 2))
```
```text
average of the two averages: 70.0
true mean of all 330 students: 61.82
```

The average of the averages (70.0) treats the 30 students of A as if they counted as much as the 300 of B. The real mean, 61.82, is much closer to B because B holds 91% of the students. The same mistake appears when averaging percentages (a 50% rate of 2 cases with a 10% rate of 1000 cases) and when averaging per-day averages over days with different numbers of events. **If you only have group means, you also need the group sizes.** Note that this cannot be done for medians at all: you cannot get the overall median from group medians; you need the data.

### 15.4 More ways a summary can mislead

- **A cut-off axis or a hidden scale** makes small differences look big. State the scale.
- **Survivorship bias**: you only see the cases that survived (the companies still in business, the students who stayed). The summary describes survivors only.
- **Percent change from a tiny base**: "sales doubled" from 2 to 4.
- **"Average" without a name.** Say mean or median.
- **Rounding too early**: round only when you print, never in between.
- **Mixed populations**: the bimodal problem of section 14.2.
- **Missing data hidden**: an average over 40% of the rows is not an average of the dataset (section 7).

## 16. A first look at correlation

Everything so far describes one variable at a time. The simplest description of **two** variables is the **correlation coefficient** `r`, a number between -1 and 1: +1 means the points lie exactly on an upward line, -1 on a downward line, 0 means no *straight-line* relationship. It is computed from the z-scores of both variables: `r` is the average of `zx x zy`. Python has it as `statistics.correlation`.

```python run
import random
import statistics

rng = random.Random(31)
hours = [rng.uniform(0, 10) for _ in range(60)]
marks = [min(100, max(0, round(35 + 5.5 * h + rng.gauss(0, 8)))) for h in hours]     # synthetic: study hours vs marks


def pearson(xs, ys):
    mx, my = statistics.fmean(xs), statistics.fmean(ys)
    sx, sy = statistics.stdev(xs), statistics.stdev(ys)
    n = len(xs)
    return sum((x - mx) / sx * (y - my) / sy for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) / (n - 1)


print("by hand           :", round(pearson(hours, marks), 4))
print("statistics module :", round(statistics.correlation(hours, marks), 4))

xs = list(range(1, 11))
ys_curve = [x ** 3 for x in xs]
print()
print("y = x ** 3 (perfectly predictable, but curved)")
print("Pearson r        :", round(statistics.correlation(xs, ys_curve), 4))
print("Spearman (ranks) :", round(statistics.correlation(xs, ys_curve, method="ranked"), 4))

ys_u = [(x - 5.5) ** 2 for x in xs]
print()
print("y = (x - 5.5) ** 2 (a perfect U shape)")
print("Pearson r        :", round(statistics.correlation(xs, ys_u), 4))
```
```text
by hand           : 0.8733
statistics module : 0.8733

y = x ** 3 (perfectly predictable, but curved)
Pearson r        : 0.9284
Spearman (ranks) : 1.0

y = (x - 5.5) ** 2 (a perfect U shape)
Pearson r        : 0.0
```

Four warnings in one block:

1. `r` = 0.87 here (a strong upward trend) is a statement about **these data**: synthetic hours against synthetic marks that were built to rise together.
2. Pearson's `r` measures *straight-line* association. A perfect curve (`x ** 3`) gives 0.9284, less than 1; the **rank** correlation (Spearman, `method="ranked"`, available in Python 3.12 and later) gives exactly 1 for any steadily increasing relationship.
3. A perfect U shape gives `r` = 0: strongly related, zero correlation. Always draw the scatter plot (you will in the matplotlib chapter).
4. **Correlation is not causation.** Ice-cream sales and drowning both rise in summer; neither causes the other.

## 17. A reusable describe() and a text report

### 17.1 Put the chapter into one function

Every section so far computed one family of numbers. Analysts need them together, in a fixed order, every time. The function `describe()` below goes into its own module, `describe.py`, so a notebook or script can `from describe import describe, report`. It returns a **dictionary** (easy to test, to print, to save as JSON) and a second function turns it into readable lines.

```python run
# file: describe.py
"""describe(): one honest summary of a list of numbers. Standard library only."""
import statistics
from collections import Counter


def describe(values):
    """Return a dict summarising `values` (a list of numbers, no None)."""
    data = list(values)                              # never mutate or trust the caller's list
    n = len(data)
    if n == 0:
        raise ValueError("describe() needs at least one value")

    mean = statistics.fmean(data)
    median = statistics.median(data)
    result = {
        "n": n,
        "min": min(data),
        "max": max(data),
        "mean": mean,
        "median": median,
        "modes": statistics.multimode(data) if len(set(data)) < n else [],
    }
    if n < 2:                                        # spread needs two values
        return result

    q1, _, q3 = statistics.quantiles(data, n=4, method="inclusive")
    sd = statistics.stdev(data)
    low_fence, high_fence = q1 - 1.5 * (q3 - q1), q3 + 1.5 * (q3 - q1)
    result.update({
        "stdev": sd,
        "q1": q1,
        "q3": q3,
        "iqr": q3 - q1,
        "mad": statistics.median(abs(x - median) for x in data),
        "cv": sd / mean if mean else None,
        "p5": statistics.quantiles(data, n=20, method="inclusive")[0],
        "p95": statistics.quantiles(data, n=20, method="inclusive")[-1],
        "outliers_low": sum(1 for x in data if x < low_fence),
        "outliers_high": sum(1 for x in data if x > high_fence),
    })
    pop_sd = statistics.pstdev(data)
    result["skewness"] = (
        statistics.fmean(((x - mean) / pop_sd) ** 3 for x in data) if pop_sd else 0.0
    )
    return result


def advice(summary):
    """One sentence on which centre to quote, based on the mean-median gap."""
    if "iqr" not in summary or summary["iqr"] == 0:
        return "Too little spread to judge the shape."
    gap = (summary["mean"] - summary["median"]) / summary["iqr"]
    if abs(gap) < 0.1 and abs(summary["skewness"]) < 0.5:
        return "Mean and median agree and the skew is small: either centre is fine."
    side = "right" if summary["skewness"] > 0 else "left"
    return f"Skewed to the {side} (skewness {summary['skewness']:.2f}): quote the median and the IQR, not only the mean."


def report(name, values, unit=""):
    """A small text report (a list of lines) for one variable."""
    s = describe(values)
    u = f" {unit}" if unit else ""
    lines = [f"{name}  (n = {s['n']})", "-" * 46]
    lines.append(f"centre : mean {s['mean']:.1f}{u}   median {s['median']:.1f}{u}")
    if "stdev" in s:
        lines.append(f"spread : stdev {s['stdev']:.1f}   IQR {s['iqr']:.1f}   MAD {s['mad']:.1f}")
        lines.append(f"range  : {s['min']} to {s['max']}   (middle 90%: {s['p5']:.1f} to {s['p95']:.1f})")
        lines.append(f"flags  : {s['outliers_low']} low and {s['outliers_high']} high values beyond the 1.5 x IQR fences")
        lines.append(f"shape  : skewness {s['skewness']:.2f}")
        lines.append("advice : " + advice(s))
    return lines
```

## 18. Reproducibility

### 18.1 Why it matters

If you cannot reproduce your own result next month, nobody else can either, and you cannot tell whether a change is real or an accident. Four things make an analysis reproducible:

1. **Seeds** for anything random, so the same code produces the same numbers.
2. **A record of the data source**: where it came from, the date you got it, its licence, and a checksum so you can tell if the file changed.
3. **A record of the environment**: Python version and library versions.
4. **A notebook or script that runs top to bottom** from the raw data, with no manual steps (section 3).

### 18.2 Seeds, and what changes when they change

Seeds make randomness repeatable. Note also what the seed teaches: different seeds give different data, so a summary of a *sample* is itself a random quantity.

```python run
import random
import statistics

for seed in [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]:
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    sample = [rng.gauss(60, 12) for _ in range(30)]
    print(f"seed {seed}: mean of 30 marks = {statistics.fmean(sample):6.2f}   median = {statistics.median(sample):6.2f}")

print()
rng_a, rng_b = random.Random(42), random.Random(42)
print("same seed twice gives identical numbers:", [rng_a.random() for _ in range(3)] == [rng_b.random() for _ in range(3)])
```
```text
seed 1: mean of 30 marks =  61.45   median =  61.99
seed 2: mean of 30 marks =  56.86   median =  55.93
seed 3: mean of 30 marks =  57.89   median =  57.53
seed 4: mean of 30 marks =  59.57   median =  60.10
seed 5: mean of 30 marks =  59.01   median =  59.28

same seed twice gives identical numbers: True
```

All five samples come from the same population (mean 60), yet the sample means move by a few marks. That spread of the *statistic* from sample to sample is the starting point of inferential statistics. For now, remember: *a small sample's mean is not the population's mean*, and any seed-dependent number in a report needs its seed written next to it.

Prefer `random.Random(seed)` (your own generator) to `random.seed(seed)` (the shared global one): another library, or another cell in a notebook, can use the global generator and change your numbers between runs.

### 18.3 A manifest: what produced this result?

A small dictionary saved next to the results records the facts. It uses `hashlib` to fingerprint the data file: if one byte changes, the fingerprint changes.

```python run
import hashlib
import json
import platform
import statistics
import sys
import tempfile
from pathlib import Path
from datasets import response_times
from describe import describe

times = response_times()
with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as folder:
    folder = Path(folder)
    data_file = folder / "response_times.csv"
    data_file.write_text("ms\n" + "\n".join(str(t) for t in times) + "\n", encoding="utf-8")

    manifest = {
        "source": "synthetic: datasets.response_times(n=500, seed=3)",
        "file": data_file.name,
        "sha256": hashlib.sha256(data_file.read_bytes()).hexdigest(),
        "rows": len(times),
        "python": platform.python_version(),
        "median_ms": round(describe(times)["median"], 1),
    }
    print(json.dumps(manifest, indent=2))

    # Change one value and see the fingerprint change.
    data_file.write_text("ms\n" + "\n".join(str(t) for t in times[:-1] + [times[-1] + 0.1]) + "\n", encoding="utf-8")
    print("fingerprint changed after editing one value:",
          hashlib.sha256(data_file.read_bytes()).hexdigest() != manifest["sha256"])
```

The `sha256` depends only on the data, so if you run the same code you should get the same value; the `python` line is specific to your machine. For real data add the **URL, the download date and the licence** to the manifest.

### 18.4 A reproducibility checklist

- Seed every random generator, and write the seed down.
- Never edit the raw data file by hand; cleaning is code that reads the raw file.
- Keep raw data separate from results.
- Record the date and source (URL) of every downloaded file.
- Record Python and library versions (`python --version`, `pip freeze`).
- Restart and run all (section 3.3) before you share.

## 20. Common traps

1. **Running cells out of order**, and trusting a notebook you never restarted (3).
2. **Mutating your data** with `.sort()` or in-place edits inside helper functions (3.4).
3. **Using `split(",")`** instead of the csv module; forgetting `newline=""` (7.3).
4. **Silent cleaning**: dropping rows without counting them or saying why (7).
5. **Averaging codes and ranks** (ordinal data), or IDs (6).
6. **Quoting the mean for skewed data**, such as income, prices and waiting times (10).
7. **Mixing up `variance` and `pvariance`**: the name without "p" divides by `n - 1` (11.3).
8. **A standard deviation of skewed data** treated as if it described a bell curve (11.5).
9. **A percentile with no method named**, or a tail percentile from a small sample (12).
10. **Deleting outliers because they are inconvenient**; or keeping an obvious typo (13.4).
11. **Believing the 3-sigma rule when outliers inflate sigma** (masking, 13.5).
12. **One histogram bin count** only; a bimodal shape lost to too few bins (14.4).
13. **Reporting one centre for bimodal data**, where the mean sits in the empty valley (14.2).
14. **Comparing overall rates of groups of different composition** (Simpson, 15.1).
15. **Averaging averages** without weights; medians of medians (15.3).
16. **Trusting identical means and standard deviations** to mean identical data (15.2).
17. **Reading a correlation as causation**, or as the absence of a relationship when `r` is near 0 (16).
18. **Unseeded randomness and no record of the data source** (18).
19. **Rounding in the middle of a calculation** instead of only when printing.
20. **Treating synthetic stand-in data as facts about the world** (5).

## 21. Summary

- **Ask the question first.** The question chooses the summary: a typical case wants a centre, a promise to most users wants a high percentile, a comparison wants groups side by side.
- **Notebooks** (Jupyter, VS Code) are for exploring and telling the story; a notebook is a JSON document plus a kernel with memory. Hidden state is the main danger: **restart and run all** before you trust or share. Move working code into `.py` modules with tests.
- **Know the kind of data**: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio. Do not average codes, ranks or identifiers.
- **Read CSVs with the `csv` module**; everything arrives as text; convert and clean in code; count kept and rejected rows; never drop rows silently.
- **Centre**: mean (balance point, good for totals and symmetric data), median (middle value, robust), mode (categories). Trimmed, weighted, geometric (growth) and harmonic (rates) means are special tools. **The median beats the mean for skewed data and outliers**, when the question is about a typical case.
- **Spread**: range, variance and standard deviation (sample divides by `n - 1`; `statistics.variance` is the sample version), IQR and MAD (robust), CV (relative). Pair mean with sd, median with IQR.
- **Percentiles** have several definitions (nearest rank, inclusive, exclusive); name the method. The five-number summary and the 1.5 x IQR fences flag outliers worth a look; z-scores can be masked by the outliers themselves, so a median/MAD version is more robust.
- **Shapes**: symmetric, right- and left-skewed, bimodal, uniform, heavy-tailed. Draw a histogram first; try more than one bin count; skewness and kurtosis are numbers for shape, but they cannot see two humps.
- **Summaries can lie**: Simpson's paradox, same-summary-different-data, averaging averages, hidden missing data. Always plot and compare groups.
- **Correlation** measures straight-line association only; it is not causation.
- **Reproducibility**: seeded `random.Random`, a manifest with source, date and checksum, raw data kept untouched, runs top to bottom.
- Reusable tools built here: `datasets.py`, `cleaning.py`, `percentiles.py`, `textplots.py`, `describe.py`.
